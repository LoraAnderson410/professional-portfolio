Curiosity + Problem Solving

Problems I Couldn't Leave Alone

Some projects are assigned. These weren't.

These started because something wasn't working, didn't make sense, or kept bothering me enough that I had to dig into it.

Sometimes that turns into a case study. Sometimes it turns into a spreadsheet for my dog. And other times, it may turn into something else. Regardless, it usually starts the same way: I want to understand what is actually going on.

Process Improvement

Turning Frustration Into a Case Study

This was not some massive months-long project. I put most of it together in a day because I had reached the point where being frustrated with the process was getting me nowhere.

Oregon's unemployment identity-verification process had me moving between portal messages, phone calls, mailed instructions, document submissions, and more waiting without always knowing what was supposed to happen next.

Eventually my operations brain took over. Instead of continuing to be annoyed, I started mapping the process, documenting where things became unclear, and thinking about what would make the experience easier for the person trying to navigate it.

In a strange way, turning it into a process problem helped. It gave my brain something useful to do with the frustration.

The result is a short case study looking at the claimant experience, communication gaps, process loops, and a few practical ways the system could be clearer.

Personal Project

Of Course I Built a Spreadsheet for My Dog

Thor has dealt with some pretty miserable skin allergies, and after trying different foods, treatments, medications, grooming changes, and everything else we could think of, I realized I was relying far too much on memory.

Was he actually scratching more this week? Did something improve after a treatment? Was the weather making it worse? Was I remembering the timing correctly?

"I think it might be helping" is not especially useful data.

So naturally, I built a spreadsheet.

The goal was simple: stop guessing, keep the information in one place, and make it easier to notice patterns over time.

The tracker gives me a way to record symptoms, treatments, food and environmental changes, and anything else that might matter. More importantly, it gives me something concrete to bring to his vet instead of trying to reconstruct weeks of observations from memory.

Is it the world's most sophisticated analytics project? No. But it solved a real problem I cared about, which is usually where my favorite projects start.

Personal Project + Data Design

Turning Scattered Symptoms Into Something Useful

This project started while I was helping a friend organize a complicated mix of symptoms, treatments, appointments, and unanswered questions. It quickly became clear that the same problem affects a lot of people.

I turned that idea into a customizable Excel tracker for symptoms, flares, treatments, labs, nutrition, triggers, appointments, diagnostic history, and barriers to care.

I designed it to be easy to use and personalize, even for someone who only knows the basics of Excel. A separate demo version includes fictional data so users can see how everything works before starting their own tracker.

What Comes Next

I Have a Feeling This Section Will Grow

I don’t really set out looking for side projects. They usually find me. Something catches my attention, I start asking questions, and before long I’ve built a spreadsheet, mapped a process, or gone much farther down a rabbit hole than I originally intended.

I’m sure these won’t be the last.

What They Have in Common

Different problem. Same instinct.

An unemployment process, a dog's allergy tracker, and an autoimmune tracking system obviously solve very different problems.

What connects them is how I tend to respond when something is confusing, frustrating, or just isn't working very well. I start organizing the information. I ask questions. I look for patterns. Eventually I usually build something.

Sometimes that becomes a formal analysis. Sometimes it becomes a tool someone else might be able to use. Sometimes it is just how I get my brain to stop spinning. Either way, I usually end up understanding the problem better than I did when I started, and with something useful to show for it.
