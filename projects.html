How I Work

I usually start with a question.

I enjoy projects that require me to figure something out. Sometimes that means working through a business process, designing a system, analyzing data, or teaching myself a new tool.

I am especially drawn to situations where information exists but is fragmented, a process technically works but creates unnecessary friction, or a problem would be easier to manage if the information were organized differently.

These projects are different in subject matter, but each one reflects the same habits: curiosity, structured problem solving, documentation, analysis, and a preference for practical solutions.

Process Improvement

When a frustrating process becomes an analysis project.

I tend to notice process friction quickly. When something is confusing, repetitive, difficult to navigate, or requires unnecessary effort, my instinct is to map what is happening and ask how it could work better.

Independent Process Analysis

Oregon Unemployment Identity Verification

A claimant-side look at process friction, exception handling, communication, and accessibility.

While navigating Oregon's unemployment process, I encountered unclear status messages, overlapping communication channels, delayed mailed instructions, sequential document requests, and an exception path that was difficult to understand.

Rather than simply documenting what went wrong, I began mapping the experience as a process-improvement case study.

The analysis looks at the claimant journey, communication gaps, handoffs, exception handling, accessibility, repeated contacts, and opportunities to reduce both claimant effort and unnecessary staff touches.

The goal is not to remove identity controls. It is to show how strong controls and a clear user experience can coexist.

Process Mapping Root Cause Analysis Risk Accessibility Service Design Continuous Improvement
  • Documented the current-state claimant journey
  • Identified friction points and failure modes
  • Evaluated communication and exception pathways
  • Developed practical future-state recommendations
  • Considered both user experience and operational burden

Sometimes the spreadsheet starts at home.

Not every useful project begins at work. Personal projects can reveal the same habits I use professionally: organize the information, create a repeatable structure, track what changes, and look for patterns instead of relying on memory.

Tracking + Analysis

Thor's Allergy Tracker

My Catahoula/Beagle mix Thor developed persistent skin and allergy symptoms with several possible contributing factors, including food, medication, grooming, environmental exposure, and weather.

Rather than relying on memory or impressions, I built a tracker to document symptoms and possible influences over time.

The goal is to make patterns easier to recognize and provide more useful information for conversations with his veterinarian.
